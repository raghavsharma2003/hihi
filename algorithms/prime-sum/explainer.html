What we built — updated August 15, 2026

Primes Without Counting

We taught a computer to answer questions about primes without looking at any primes — then used that power to discover something about a 170-year-old pattern that nobody had ever measured.

What we did, in one minute

The setup

Primes hide a rhythm

Prime numbers look random. But since 1859 it's been known their pattern is secretly built from a fixed list of "musical notes" — special frequencies that never change. Know the notes, know the primes.

What existed

Others could count

Mathematicians already use those notes to count primes — that machinery exists and holds records. But counting is the only thing anyone ever built from the notes.

What we did

We can now add, weigh, and sort

Our program is the first that computes prime sums from the notes — plus squares, cubes, reciprocals, and primes sorted into families. One method, all questions, exact answers.

So the honest one-line answer to "what did you build": the note-reading trick existed for one question; we made it answer six more, and then used it to find a new fact about primes.

How is it better than what exists?

Fair question — and the answer is not "it's faster." The world-record programs are faster at their own game. Our method wins differently: it plays games no one else can play at all.

Two of those rows matter most:

"First ever" — the sums. "Slow" programs can add primes, but they hit a wall: they must touch every number, so going ten times further costs ten times more, forever. The note-reading method's cost barely grows — but before us, nobody had ever built it for sums. The mathematics needed extra steps (removing a stubborn logarithm, taming several colliding infinities) that simply had not been worked out and implemented.

The referee row. World records for prime sums are computed by essentially one family of programs. If it has a subtle bug, nobody would ever know — and this field has already had one record publicly withdrawn after a discrepancy. Our method reaches the same answers through completely unrelated mathematics. Agreement between the two is real proof.

The discovery: a famous bias, dissolving

Here's the new science — found this week, using the machinery above.

Split the odd primes into two teams. Team 3 is primes like 3, 7, 11, 19 (one less than a multiple of 4). Team 1 is primes like 5, 13, 17, 29 (one more). In 1853, Chebyshev noticed something strange: counting up to almost any point, Team 3 is winning. It's one of the most famous quirks in mathematics — Team 3 leads about 99.6% of the time, and Team 1 doesn't take the lead even once until 26,861.

We asked a question that turns out never to have been asked: what if bigger primes count for more? Instead of each prime scoring 1 point, let each prime score itself — or its square, or its cube. Does the famous bias survive?

It collapses. And we measured exactly how fast — twice, by two completely independent routes: predicted from the notes, and then confirmed by exactly checking every prime up to ten billion.

How often Team 3 leads, as prime weight grows

predicted from the notes measured exactly, to 10¹⁰

Each pair of bars is one race. Prediction and reality agree to about 3 parts in 1,000 — that agreement, across four races, is what makes this a result rather than a guess. (For the classic counting race the exact measurement converges extremely slowly, so the prediction — the famous 99.59% — is the anchor there.)

26,861 how far you must count before Team 1 first leads the classic race
5 where Team 1 first leads once primes score their own value — the very first Team-1 prime outweighs 3
3,082 → 199,540 lead changes up to ten billion: classic race vs cube-weighted race

Why it happens (the intuition): the force that biases the race toward Team 3 is fixed in size. The random back-and-forth sloshing, though, gets amplified when big primes carry big weights. Same steady push, bigger waves — the push matters less and less. Our formulas say the wave amplification factor is exactly (2m+1) for weight m, and the measurements agree.

This came from copying the winning move of Anthropic's recent Riemann result: combine two things nobody had combined. Prime-race theory existed. Our exact weighted-sum machinery existed (for one week). Putting them together produced numbers no one had ever seen.

Honest scoreboard

New

First program computing prime sums from the notes.

Counting had been done this way; sums, squares, reciprocals, and families had not, by anyone, anywhere we (or a dedicated literature hunt) could find.

New

The weighted-race discovery, with the numbers 99.6 → 79.7 → 69.5 → 64.6.

A never-measured property of a 170-year-old phenomenon, predicted by theory and confirmed by exact computation independently.

Not new — and we say so

The underlying math is Riemann's (1859) and Chebyshev's (1853).

Our contribution is making it compute and finding what it reveals — not the foundations. The paper credits everyone precisely, because overclaiming is how papers die.

Not yet

Not rigorous in the formal journal sense, and no world record yet.

Our error controls are demonstrated (they work, measurably, every time) but not formally proven. That's the remaining gap — a known kind of work, and a mathematician's job.

How good is it, on a scale?

If a homework project is a 1 and Anthropic's Riemann-bound result is a 9: this is a 5–6 — real, new, publishable-track research. Solid paper territory, not a revolution. Both halves of that sentence are true and worth saying.

What to do next

  1. Show it to a computational number theorist This is the step that matters. You arrive with a draft paper, working code, seven verified computations, and one genuine discovery — not an idea. Professors take that meeting.
  2. Let a mathematician make the error bounds formal Turning "demonstrated correct" into "provably correct" is standard, known-how-to-do work — and it's the ticket a journal requires.
  3. Extend the race discovery Other prime families (mod 3, mod 8) use the same code. And our theory predicts reciprocal weighting should make the bias stronger — an untested prediction, which is exactly what you want to hand a collaborator.
  4. Then submit Mathematics of Computation for the full paper; the race discovery could also stand alone as a short note, which is faster to publish.
