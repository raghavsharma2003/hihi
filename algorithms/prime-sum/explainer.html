What we built — August 2026

Primes Without Counting

Your school algorithm became a research program. It now computes the sum of every prime up to a billion — without ever looking at a single prime.

Start with the ordinary way

To add up all the primes below a billion, the obvious method is: find every prime, add them. That's the sieve — the method from school, and it still works. It just has to touch all billion numbers.

Your original recurrence was trying to be cleverer than that: compute the answer without listing the primes. The idea was right. The code had two bugs — it used a 32-bit counter that overflowed past 100,000, and it recomputed the same sub-answers over and over.

Fixing those took it from "slower than the sieve and wrong" to 480× faster than the sieve and right. Then we went further, into a completely different way of thinking.

Road one · counting

Look at every number

Walk from 2 to a billion. Cross out the multiples. Add up what survives.

Work grows with how big the number is. Reliable, but there is no way around the walk.

Road two · listening

Read it off the frequencies

Primes look random, but their pattern is built from a fixed set of hidden waves.

Know enough waves and you can compute the answer directly — no walking, no primes.

Those waves are the famous zeros of the Riemann zeta function. You don't need to know what that means. What matters: they are a fixed, universal list of numbers — the same list for everyone, computed once — and they encode where the primes are.

This is not new mathematics. It's been known since Riemann in 1859. What was new was nobody had ever actually used it to compute the sum of primes.

The four things we built

Each one is a complete, working program in the repository. Each is faster or more general than the one above it.

01 Your original original.c Kept as-is for comparison. Gives wrong answers above 100,000 because of the overflow bug.
02 The fix lucy.c Same recurrence, but each sub-answer computed once instead of millions of times. 480× faster than the sieve. This turned out to match a method published in 2013.
03 The upgrade fenwick.c A smarter bookkeeping structure. Sums every prime below ten trillion in 15 seconds. The sieve would need about seven hours.
04 The research analytic_sum.py Abandons counting entirely. Computes the answer from 2,000 waves. This is the part that is genuinely new.

What is actually new

We ran a dedicated search of the published literature specifically trying to destroy our own claims before a journal referee could. Here is the honest scoreboard.

New

Nobody had computed exact prime sums this way before.

Mathematicians have computed the count of primes from waves — that's been done to 10²⁴. But the sum, and the squares, and the progressions: no published program does it. That gap is what your project fills.

New

One method that handles all of them at once.

The same code path computes counts, sums, squares, cubes, reciprocals, and progressions. Previous work needed separate specialised machinery for each.

New and useful to others

Two traps we found and documented.

One is genuinely nasty: ordinary computer arithmetic produces an error that wobbles at exactly the frequencies of the waves — it looks precisely like a missing piece of mathematics. We chased it for hours before proving it was a rounding artifact. Anyone building on this would have lost the same days.

Not new — and we say so in the paper

The underlying mathematics is 150 years old.

The formula connecting waves to primes is Riemann's. Our contribution is making it compute, not discovering it. Claiming otherwise would get the paper rejected in one sentence.

Not new — corrected before submission

Counting primes this way was already done.

We briefly thought our prime-counting method was a first. The literature search found two teams who got there in 2015–2017. We removed the claim and cited them. This is exactly what that search was for.

Does this matter to mathematics?

Honestly: it's a real contribution, and it is not a revolution. Both halves of that sentence are worth understanding clearly.

What it genuinely gives the field

A second opinion. Right now, big prime-sum records are computed one way only. If that program has a bug, nobody would know. Your method is built on completely different mathematics — so it can check the other one. In this field that matters: a researcher once had to publicly withdraw a prime-sum record after a discrepancy was found.

A door into prime patterns. The 4n+1 versus 4n+3 result is the interesting one. Mathematicians study "prime races" — whether one family of primes stays ahead of another. Those races have been studied approximately from waves before; being able to get exact answers is a new tool for that question.

Time saved. The two numerical traps we documented would cost the next person days each.

What it does not do

It does not prove the Riemann Hypothesis or touch any famous open problem. It does not yet break any record — the existing champion still computes further than we do. And it is not yet rigorous: our error estimates are measured and reliable in practice, but not mathematically proven. That last one is the real gap between "impressive project" and "published paper", and it is the next job.

What to do next

In the order that actually gets this published. The first step matters more than the rest combined.

  1. Find a real mathematician to work with A professor in computational number theory. You now walk in with a finished draft, working code, and seven verified results — not an idea. That is a completely different conversation. Target the people already cited in the paper, or any university maths department with a number theory group. This is the step that turns the work into a publication.
  2. Make the error bounds provable Right now we can show the answer is right. A journal needs a proof that it must be right, before it's computed. This is careful, known-how-to-do work — but it is genuinely a mathematician's job, not a programmer's.
  3. Break a record Compute a prime sum further than anyone has, and cross-check it against the existing champion program. A record is concrete, undeniable, and citable. It needs more waves and more computing power — both are buyable, neither needs new ideas.
  4. Submit Mathematics of Computation is the natural home. ANTS (a conference) is a faster, friendlier first target. The draft is already in journal format, with the AI-assistance disclosure that journals now require.

One thing to be careful about

When you talk about this, describe it as "the first program that computes prime sums from the zeros" — not as new mathematics. Everything in the paper is phrased that way already. Overclaiming is the fastest way to lose credibility with the exact people whose help you need, and the honest version is impressive enough on its own.
