Your research, worked out with a pencil

Prime Races by Hand

Every number on this page is small enough to check yourself. By the end you will have watched the famous bias flip — using nine primes and one subtraction.

01

Sorting primes into two teams

Take any odd prime and divide it by 4. There's always a remainder, and it's always either 1 or 3 — never 0 or 2, because those would make the number even.

So every odd prime belongs to exactly one of two teams. Check a few:

02

The flip, in nine primes

Here is the entire idea of the research, and you can verify it on paper in about a minute.

Stop at 29. Team 3 has 3, 7, 11, 19, 23 — that's five primes. Team 1 has 5, 13, 17, 29 — four primes. So if you're counting, Team 3 is winning, 5 to 4.

Now add them up instead of counting them.

Nothing changed except the scoring rule. Same nine primes, same moment on the number line — and the answer to "who is winning?" reverses. That reversal is what the whole paper is about.

Why did it flip? Look at who's on each team. Team 1's primes here are the bigger ones — it owns 29, the largest in the range. Team 3 has more primes, but they're smaller. Counting rewards how many; adding rewards how big. The two rules can genuinely disagree.

03

What the weight actually is

"Counting", "adding", and "adding squares" aren't three separate ideas. They're one idea with a knob on it.

Give each prime p a score of pm, where m is the number you choose. That's the weight:

The same knob, four settingswhat the prime 7 scores
weight meach prime scoresprime 7 scoresthis is called
0p⁰ = 11counting them
1p¹ = p7adding them
2p²49adding the squares
3p³343adding the cubes

Anything to the power zero is 1 — that's why m = 0 turns the weighted race back into plain counting. The classical 170-year-old problem is just one setting of this knob.

And the knob doesn't have to stop at whole numbers, or at zero. Negative weights make small primes worth more: at m = −1 the prime 3 scores 1/3 while 29 scores only 1/29. That whole negative side is the part mathematicians had already been studying — from the other end, without anyone connecting it to the counting problem.

04

Where the bias comes from

Before explaining why weight destroys the bias, here's why the bias exists at all. It's a genuinely lovely piece of arithmetic.

Square any odd prime and divide by 4:

Squares of primesthe remainder is always 1
primesquareddivided by 4remainder

Always 1. Never 3. Try any odd prime you like — it can't fail, because an odd number is 2k+1, and (2k+1)² = 4(k²+k) + 1.

So squares of primes always land on Team 1's side.

Mathematicians don't just count primes — for technical reasons they count primes and their powers together, and that fuller count comes out almost perfectly balanced. But the squares are all handing free points to Team 1. For the books to balance, the plain primes must lean the other way, toward Team 3.

That lean is Chebyshev's bias. It's not mystical: it's the primes compensating for a systematic bonus the squares give Team 1. And crucially, it's a steady lean — the same relative size no matter how far out you go.

05

Why the weight destroys it

Now the mechanism. Two forces fight in this race:

The lean — steady, always the same relative size, from the squares.

The wobble — the race jitters around because primes arrive irregularly.

Weighting doesn't touch the lean. It makes the wobble enormous. Here's the reason, in one table: as the weight rises, a handful of the biggest primes swallow the entire score.

At m = 8, ten primes out of 167 decide half the race. Whether those ten happen to be Team 3 or Team 1 is essentially a coin toss — and a coin toss easily drowns out a small steady lean.

That's the whole explanation. When you count, thousands of primes each contribute a tiny amount, all the small random fluctuations average out, and the steady lean shows through cleanly: Team 3 leads 99.6% of the time.

When you weight heavily, a few giants dominate. The lean is still there, exactly as strong as before — but it's now a whisper in a storm. The race becomes a coin flip.

Sliding toward 50% — a fair coin — but never quite reaching it. The lean never fully dies; it just gets buried deeper and deeper.

06

The thing people get wrong

It's tempting to think the bias fades as numbers get bigger. It doesn't. This is worth being very clear about, because it's the single most misunderstood point.

"Team 3 leads 99.6% of the time" is a statement about forever — the fraction of the whole infinite number line. Go to a trillion, a quintillion, further: still 99.6%. There is no crossover point where Team 1 catches up.

What does change the answer is the weight. Two different knobs entirely — one does nothing to the bias, the other dissolves it.

One honest complication 99.6% doesn't mean Team 3 always leads. Team 1 does take the lead sometimes — the first time is at 26,861, and it happens infinitely often. You saw exactly this in the scoreboard above: the sum column flipped to Team 1 six times before 47. Rare, but real, and never stopping.
07

How we actually computed it

You can check a race up to 47 by hand. Up to ten billion, a computer can sieve it directly — we did, and that's where the measured numbers come from. But "99.6% of the time, forever" is about infinity, and no computer reaches infinity.

So there's a second route, and it's the strange and beautiful part.

The irregularity of the primes — the wobble — isn't random noise. It's literally a sum of waves. Each wave has a frequency, and those frequencies are the famous zeros of the Riemann zeta function (and its relatives). This is Riemann's discovery from 1859, and it's why the Riemann Hypothesis matters so much: those frequencies control how irregular the primes can be.

Once you know the frequencies, you can compute how often the wobble beats the lean — without ever counting a single prime. That's what produces the 99.6%, the 79.8%, all of it. We computed 511 of those frequencies ourselves, to 25 decimal places.

And the two routes agree. The wave calculation predicts 79.76% for weight 1; sieving every prime to ten billion measured 79.38%. Two completely different methods, same answer. That agreement is the strongest evidence in the paper.

08

What we proved

Computing numbers is one thing; proving statements about all weights at once is another. The paper has four theorems. In plain words:

The four theoremseach proved for every real weight, not just the ones we computed
One The percentage is a real, well-defined number at every weight, and it slides smoothly as you turn the knob — no jumps, no gaps. smooth
Two It dissolves toward 50% at a speed we can write down and prove — not just observe. speed
Three Mathematicians already knew the bias fades if you change the number system instead of the weight. Those are the same theorem. unified
Four Past a certain weight the bias only ever decreases — it can never wobble back up. one-way

Theorem four is proved for weights above about 17; below that it's a conjecture backed by 30 computed points. The paper says so.

There's also the other half of the project: computing the exact sum of every prime below a billion — 24,739,512,092,254,535 — from those wave frequencies rather than from the primes. People had reconstructed the count of primes that way before. The sum hadn't been done.

09

What's assumed, and what's still open

Everything about the percentages rests on two unproved assumptions: the Riemann Hypothesis (for these related functions), and a technical independence property of those wave frequencies. That sounds alarming and isn't — every paper in this field, going back to the 1994 work that established the 99.6%, stands on the same two. The paper states it plainly on page one.

The sieve computations to ten billion assume nothing at all. They're just counting, and they land on top of the predictions.

Genuinely open, and listed in the paper: monotonicity below weight 17; one corner where two limits interact; and the final numerical integration step, which is high-precision floating point rather than fully certified arithmetic. Everything else in the numerics — the constants, the frequencies, their completeness — is now machine-proved.
