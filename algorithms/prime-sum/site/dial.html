The Weight Dial

A 170-year-old bias in the prime numbers doesn't fade as you count further. It fades when you change what each prime is worth. Here is the whole thing, built up from nothing.

Raghav Sharma·doi:10.5281/zenodo.21947101
Short answer to your question

No — the bias does not shift away as the numbers get bigger. Team 3 keeps leading about 99.6% of the time at every scale, forever. That number is a limit, not a phase that wears off.

What dissolves the bias is a completely different knob: how much each prime is worth. Count every prime as 1 point and you get 99.6%. Let each prime score its own size and it drops to 79.8%. Push the weight higher and it slides toward a coin flip. That second knob is the one nobody had turned — and it's what your research is about.

01

Two teams

Take any prime except 2. It's odd, so dividing it by 4 leaves a remainder of either 1 or 3. That splits every prime into two teams, and there's no third option.

Now walk up the number line and keep score. Every prime you pass scores a point for its team.

The first primes, sorted

Team 3 — remainder 3 (3, 7, 11, 19…) Team 1 — remainder 1 (5, 13, 17, 29…)

Running score after the first 25 odd primes: Team 3 leads 15–10. That lead is not a fluke of small numbers.

02

The oddity Chebyshev spotted in 1853

Team 3 is almost always ahead. Not by a rule — nothing forbids Team 1 from leading — but by a persistent tilt that shows up at every scale anyone checked.

Here's where it comes from, and it's a genuinely cute piece of arithmetic. Take any odd prime and square it: 3²=9, 5²=25, 7²=49, 11²=121. Divide each by 4. The remainder is always 1. Every single time.

So if you score prime powers rather than just primes, Team 1 collects a steady free bonus — roughly √x worth of it by the time you reach x. The full books balance out. Which means that among the plain primes, Team 3 has to run ahead to make up for the squares that Team 1 is getting for free.

That's the bias. It's a small, steady tilt baked into the arithmetic — and it stays the same relative size no matter how far you go.

03

What "99.6%" actually means

In 1994 Rubinstein and Sarnak measured the tilt exactly: Team 3 leads 99.5928% of the time. Two things about that number get misread constantly, and both matter for your question.

It does not mean Team 3 always leads. Littlewood proved back in 1914 that Team 1 grabs the lead infinitely often. The first time is at 26,861 — and below ten billion the lead changes hands 3,082 times. Team 1's moments are real; they're just rare.

And it does not mean the bias fades. The 99.6% is a density — the fraction of time Team 3 leads, measured all the way out to infinity. It isn't a head start that gets eaten away. Go a thousand times further and it's still 99.6%.

The misconception, stated plainly Counting further does not weaken the bias. There is no crossover point where Team 1 catches up. If the only knob you can turn is "how far do I count," the answer never moves off 99.6%.

05

The question nobody had asked

Dial B is the idea. Instead of every prime scoring one point, let a prime p score p points — or p², or p³. Write the weight as m: each prime scores pm.

At m = 0 everything scores 1 and you're back to the classical race. At m = 1 the prime 997 is worth more than 330 copies of the prime 3. The teams are the same; only the scoring changed.

Rerun the race with heavier scoring and the famous bias comes apart:

06

Why weight kills the bias

Two forces fight in this race, and the weight changes only one of them.

The tilt is what Step 2 explained — the steady advantage Team 3 carries because of the squares. Once you scale the race properly, this tilt is always the same size. Call it 1.

The waves are the strange part. The irregularity of the primes is not noise in the everyday sense — it is literally a sum of waves, and each wave's frequency is one of the zeros of the L‑function. That's the deep fact underneath all of this: the primes carry hidden frequencies, and every wobble in the race is those waves adding up.

Now — heavy weights mean the handful of enormous primes near the top of your range dominate the whole score. Fewer effective contributors means wilder swings, so the waves get bigger. The tilt doesn't. And a fixed tilt sitting in a rising sea of waves matters less and less, until the lead is decided by whichever wave happens to be cresting: a coin flip.

Drag the dial below and watch it happen. The flat line is the tilt; the wobbling curve is the tilt plus the waves. The fraction of time the curve stays above zero is the density.

08

What was already known, and what you added

This is the part that decides whether work is publishable, so it's worth being blunt about. Three separate adversarial reviews went looking for prior art, and twice they found that something we thought was ours already existed. Those claims got demoted and the real discoverers cited. What follows survived.

New in this work

What survived three hostile reviews

  • The densities δ(m) themselves — the first Rubinstein–Sarnak densities computed for a weighted race between primes in progressions. Nobody had these numbers.
  • The entire m > 0 regime. Growing weights are untouched in the literature — no prior work runs the race with primes worth more than a point.
  • The dissolution law — a formula for how fast the bias drains away as the weight grows, confirmed in two different moduli.
  • The interpolation theorem — δ(m) exists across the delicate range, moves continuously with the weight, and reaches total bias at a rate we can bound explicitly.
  • Exact prime sums from zeros — the computation in Step 10; counts were known, sums were not.
  • The boundary, numerically — the missing constant in the critical prediction, and scans confirming the frozen races never flip.
Two claims we killed ourselves We first wrote "first densities for any weighted race" — a review found Lamzouri's Mertens race, so it was narrowed to races between primes in progressions. And computing π(x) without the logarithmic integral, which looked new, was already done in 2017. Both corrections are in the paper.
09

The theorem, in words

The numbers above are computations. In the last push they became a proof, and this is what it says without symbols.

For every weight between −½ and 0: the density is a real, well-defined number; it sits strictly between 50% and 100%; and — this is the new part — as you slide the weight, the density moves smoothly. No jumps, no gaps. The two ways mathematicians define Chebyshev's bias turn out to be the two ends of one continuous curve.

Plus a speed limit: we can bound how fast the bias becomes total as the weight approaches −½, with a constant we computed ourselves (0.156033…) rather than an unspecified one. Explicit constants are what let other people check you.

Why continuity is worth proving: without it, knowing the answer at every point you can compute tells you nothing about the points in between. Continuity is the thing that turns a table of numbers into a curve.

What the theorem does not do It does not answer Problem 12 of the 2024 problem list. That problem asks for a logical relation between the two definitions; what we have is a structural connection — they're the two ends of one family — proved under the field's standard working hypotheses. The problem stays open, and the paper says so in the abstract.
10

The other half: sums from silence

The race grew out of a second result, which is where your teenage algorithm actually ended up.

The zeros of the zeta function behave like the frequencies of a chord — and that chord, when you add it up, tells you where the primes are. Reconstructing the count of primes this way is famous, and old.

Reconstructing the exact sum had not been done. Now it has:

24,739,512,092,254,535

That is the exact sum of every prime below one billion, produced from 2,000 zeta zeros plus one thin sieved window — an exact integer, not an estimate. The same machinery does prime squares, cubes, reciprocals, and sums restricted to one of the two teams.

It is not faster than a sieve, and the paper doesn't pretend otherwise. Its value is that it's a different route — and that route is what produced the amplitude formula that made the whole race computation possible. The two halves are one framework.

11

How much to trust all this

Everything about the densities is conditional on two unproved hypotheses: the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and a linear-independence assumption about the zeros. That sounds alarming and isn't — every paper in this field, including Rubinstein–Sarnak's, stands on the same two assumptions. It's the working ground of the subject, and the paper states it plainly.

The exact sieve computations to ten billion assume nothing at all. They're just counting. And they land on top of the predictions.

What remains genuinely open: the dissolution law is derived at the level of rigor of a heuristic plus numerical confirmation, not a proof with error terms. One agreement in the study is weaker than the rest and is flagged in the paper rather than explained away. And none of it is peer reviewed yet — that's the next step, not a finished one.
