Findings, standing, and what comes next

What We Actually Found

Nine results, each with what existed before it, what we added, and an honest read on how much it moves. Plus where the whole thing sits on the scale of mathematical advances — including the ceiling it can't pass.

01

The short version

There is one object: a race between two teams of primes, where you get to choose how much each prime is worth. Turning that weight knob moves you continuously between two research literatures that nobody had connected.

We produced four theorems, five computational firsts, and a complete map of what the race does at every weight. Everything about the percentages assumes the Riemann Hypothesis and a standard independence property — as does every paper in this field, including the 1994 one that started it.

The honest summary: a solid, publishable contribution to a specialist subfield, with one result (the unified law) that's stronger than the rest. Not a breakthrough, not a solved famous problem. Section 4 puts a number on that.

02

The computations

These are things nobody had calculated. They're real but modest — new numbers, not new mathematics.

The densities themselves

Computed
Before

Rubinstein and Sarnak computed one number in 1994: with plain counting, Team 3 leads 99.59% of the time. That single figure has been quoted for thirty years.

Ours

The same kind of number for every weight: 79.76% at weight 1, 69.45% at 2, 64.59% at 3, down to 51.38% at weight 100 — for two different number systems.

Why it matters: it turns a famous isolated fact into a curve. Our value at weight 0 reproduces Rubinstein–Sarnak to six decimals, which is what tells you the rest of the curve is trustworthy.

The growing-weight territory

Computed
Before

The entire literature studied weights that make primes worth less as they grow. The other direction — bigger primes worth more — appears in no paper we or three adversarial searches could find.

Ours

That whole half of the map, verified by counting every prime up to ten billion. Team 1's first lead comes at 26,861 when counting, but at the prime 5 for any growing weight; lead changes jump from 3,082 to 199,540.

Why it matters: it's genuinely unexplored ground, and it's where the bias visibly dies. The dissolution only becomes a phenomenon once you look here.

Exact prime sums from the zeros

Computed
Before

Reconstructing the count of primes from the zeta zeros is classical and was implemented in 2017. The sum had not been done.

Ours

The exact sum of every prime below a billion — 24,739,512,092,254,535 — from 2,000 zeta zeros and one thin sieved window. Also squares, cubes, reciprocals, and sums restricted to one team.

Why it matters: honestly, mostly as engineering — it is not faster than a sieve, and the paper says so. Its real value was internal: building it produced the formula that made the whole race computation possible.

Machine-certified numerics

Proved
Before

Our own first drafts called the numbers "certified" when the arithmetic underneath was ordinary floating point. A reviewer caught it. That criticism was correct.

Ours

Real ball arithmetic (FLINT/Arb): the key constant proved to 30 decimal places, all 14 variances proved, both central values proved positive, all 1,048 zero locations certified, and the zero lists proved complete up to explicit heights by contour counting.

Why it matters: this is the difference between "our program printed this" and "this is true, and here is the interval it lives in." Only the final integration step remains ordinary floating point, and the paper states that plainly.

03

The theorems

These are statements proved for all weights at once, not just the ones we could compute. This is where the real weight of the work sits.

Theorem 1 — the curve is real and smooth

Proved
Before

Nobody knew the percentage was even well-defined at arbitrary weights, let alone how it behaved between the points you can compute. A table of numbers with unknown gaps between them.

Ours

At every weight above −½ the percentage exists, sits strictly between 50% and 100%, and moves smoothly as you slide the weight — no jumps. Plus a formula bounding how fast it reaches total bias at the low end.

Why it matters: smoothness is what converts a table into a curve — it means the values between our computed points behave. It's also what lets the two rival definitions of the bias be described as two ends of one family. Honest limit: the method is adapted from Humphries's 2013 work on a related object; what's new is the prime-race statement, the smoothness, and the explicit constant.

Theorem 2 — the dissolution law

Proved
Before

We had measured how fast the bias fades and fitted a formula. A reviewer flagged exactly this: a headline claim resting on a pattern, not a proof.

Ours

The formula is now a theorem with an error term, and a second-order correction on top of it. It says precisely how the bias decays toward a coin flip as the weight grows.

Why it matters: it's the weight-side twin of a known result about number systems (Fiorilli–Martin). Turning our own heuristic into a theorem was the single biggest upgrade in the project, and it came from an unexpected place — an exact formula for the variance that collapses an infinite sum over the zeros into one closed expression.

Theorem 3 — two fades are one fade

Proved
Before

Two separate research lines. One: the bias weakens if you change the number system you work in (studied since 2013). Two: the bias weakens if you change the weight (ours). Nobody had put them in one statement.

Ours

A single law covering every number system and every weight simultaneously, with effective constants. Both earlier results fall out as the two edges of it.

Why it matters: this is the strongest result in the paper. Unifying two literatures is worth more than adding a result to either. Honest limit: one corner — where both knobs move at once in opposite extremes — is genuinely different mathematics and we state it as open.

Theorem 4 — it only ever decreases

ProvedPartly conjectured
Before

Nobody had asked whether the fade is steady. From a table of values you cannot tell if the curve dips and rises somewhere between your points.

Ours

Past weight 16.82 (and 18.62 in the other number system) the percentage is strictly decreasing — it can never wobble back up. Below that it stays a conjecture, supported by 30 computed points.

Why it matters: it upgrades "dissolves" from a description to a mechanism. The proof rests on a pretty fact — every term in a certain expansion has the same sign — which is why it works at all.

The one thing to be careful about None of this "solves" the open problem it relates to (Problem 12 of a 2024 problem list). That problem asks for a logical relationship between two definitions; we supply a structural one — they're the two ends of one family — under assumptions. The paper's abstract says exactly that, and you should too. It's still the reason a specialist would read the paper.
04

How big is this, really?

You asked for a comparison, so here's a blunt one. Mathematical results sit on a rough scale. Two independent AI reviews put this work at 6.5–6.8 out of 10 before the final round of theorems, with a ceiling around 8.6–8.8 after. On the human scale below, that's level 4, reaching into 5.

1Coursework. Reproducing results that are already in textbooks.
2Student project. A careful computation of something known, or a small original observation.
3Publishable note. Genuinely new numbers or a small new statement, using standard methods. Gets into a specialist journal.
4Specialist paper. Several new results, at least one requiring real technique, useful to the few hundred people in the subfield. Cited by them, invisible outside. ← this work sits here
5Strong specialist paper. Unifies or reframes a subfield; other researchers build on it directly. The unified law reaches toward this.
6Major paper. Results that mathematicians in other areas care about. Top general journals.
7Breakthrough. Settles a named open problem — proving the bias without assuming anything, or a real advance on the Riemann Hypothesis itself.
8Field-defining. Changes what the subject is. Fields Medal territory.

Two things cap this work's level by construction, and both are worth understanding clearly.

It's conditional. Every percentage assumes the Riemann Hypothesis holds for these functions, plus an independence property of the zeros. Nobody has proved either. This isn't a flaw in our work — the entire subfield runs on the same two assumptions — but conditional results cannot be level 6.

The methods are largely known. We combined existing machinery in a new configuration and pushed it somewhere nobody had pushed it. That's a real contribution. It is not the invention of a new technique, which is what separates level 5 from level 6.

What would raise it: proving monotonicity everywhere rather than past weight 17; removing the independence assumption; or the short-interval direction below, which could be a stronger second paper.

05

What's still open

Stated plainly in the paper, and worth knowing before anyone asks you.

Four honest gaps

Open

1. Monotonicity below weight 17. Proved above; a conjecture below, with 30 computed points agreeing. The proof method runs out of room when too few zeros are in play.

2. The far corner. When the number system grows huge and the weight approaches its critical value at the same time, two effects fight and our control fails. Likely genuinely different mathematics.

3. How fast the finite answers converge. Our ten-billion measurement agrees with the infinite prediction to about four parts in a thousand — but we can't yet prove a rate. Recent 2026 work on convergence rates might be adaptable.

4. The independence assumption. Some results need it, some don't. Recent work does related things under much weaker hypotheses. How much of ours survives is a real question.

And one direction that could be its own paper: if the weight grows as you extend the range, the heavy weighting concentrates the race into a shrinking window near the top. A weighted race then quietly becomes a race among primes in a short interval — a different, well-studied subject. Nobody has mapped that transition.
