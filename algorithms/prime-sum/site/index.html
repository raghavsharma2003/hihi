The bias that dissolves

Primes of the form 4n+3 outrun primes of the form 4n+1 about 99.6% of the time — a curiosity noticed in 1853 and unexplained for a century. Change how much each prime is worth, and that famous bias comes apart in a way you can watch.

What the research contains

Four theorems and five computational firsts, all conditional on the standard hypotheses of the field.

The curve is smooth

At every weight the percentage exists, sits strictly between 50% and 100%, and slides without jumps.

The dissolution law

Exactly how fast the bias fades toward a coin flip, proved with an error term and a second-order correction.

Two fades are one

Changing the number system and changing the weight turn out to be the same theorem, unified in one uniform law.

It never comes back

Past a certain weight the bias only ever decreases — proved above weight 17, conjectured below.

Sums from the zeros

The exact sum of every prime below a billion, reconstructed from 2,000 zeta zeros rather than from the primes.

Machine-certified

Constants proved to 30 decimal places and 1,048 zero locations certified in ball arithmetic, not just computed.

The paper

A 59-page preprint, openly licensed, with all code and data. It states what is proved, what is computed, and what remains open — including four gaps we could not close.

Results involving percentages assume the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and a standard independence property of the zeros, as does every paper in this area. The computations up to ten billion assume nothing.
